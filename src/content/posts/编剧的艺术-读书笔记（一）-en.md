## What This Book Is About

This is a classic work that deeply explores the process of writing film and television scripts. The author reveals the key to crafting compelling, emotionally resonant stories through detailed techniques on plot construction, character development, and dialogue writing. It was also my first book on screenwriting, and there are so many points worth recording and reflecting on. I'm sharing them here, and I hope you find them helpful.

The following are notes I compiled during reading — not a verbatim copy of the original. To make it easier for myself to understand later, I've adjusted some wording and context.

## 1. Premise: The Purpose of a Play

Without a clearly structured premise, no inspiration or situation is strong enough to guide your drama toward a logical conclusion.

Always write about what you believe in. Your premise should first be your own conviction, so you can commit yourself fully to proving it. Perhaps your premise seems absurd to me, but you must prove it as your truth.

Not all examples carry high literary value, but they have well-defined premises — and that is essential for all good writing. Without a premise, you cannot delve into your characters.

**A premise must contain: character, conflict, and resolution.** If the premise is not clear and specific, these elements will remain unknown.

A premise does not need to be a universal truth. Poverty does not always lead to crime. But if you choose this premise, it is the truth within your work.

No part of a script should have an independent life — all elements must blend into a harmonious whole.

## 2. Character

### Framework

Three dimensions of a character: physiological, psychological, sociological.

Studying a character's traits, beliefs, habits, good and evil is far from enough. We need to understand why a person has become who they are. Whether voluntarily or not, their character will inevitably keep changing.

Every action a character takes is closely tied to a specific moment in their past.

The author must understand all three dimensions of a character, but does not necessarily need to mention them in the script. These traits will be conveyed through the character's actions. You must know every detail about your characters and how they would behave in every situation. Everything that happens in your play must stem directly from the characters you have chosen, and they must have a strong will to help you prove the premise.

### Environment

In fact, even a simple "yes" or "no" response is the result of a complex, subtle examination, evaluation, and adjustment of our fictional or real environment, our psychological or physiological state, and our economic conditions.

Our thoughts are often constrained by external influences — or swayed by environmental factors — no less than our physiological reactions. Just like those early memories that seem to have been forgotten yet remain deeply rooted in our minds, no matter how hard we try to escape the influence of the past or discipline our nature, we cannot break free from its grip. No matter how hard we strive to be impartial, this unconscious memory always influences our judgment.

A character is the sum of their physiological conditions and the impact of their environment.

### Dialectics

The development of dialogue consists of three parts:

First, a statement of a proposition — the thesis.

Then, the opposite of that proposition — the antithesis.

Finally, to resolve the opposition, the original proposition must be revised to form a synthesis.

These three steps follow a logical law of motion. Everything in continuous motion is constantly negating itself. Through motion, everything transforms into its opposite — the present becomes the past, the future becomes the present. Nothing exists in a state of complete stillness.

Change is the driving force that compels things to move, and movement itself becomes different from what it was. The past becomes the present, and together they determine the future.

### Character Growth

A character's stance must be revealed through conflict. Conflict begins with a decision made by a character, a decision based on the premise. The character's decision inevitably triggers a corresponding decision from the opponent. These decisions keep generating, driving the drama toward its ultimate goal — proving its premise.

A character needs many forces before making a decision, primarily from physiological, psychological, and sociological dimensions. Based on these, countless motivations can be created.

Only in bad writing do characters change in ways that contradict their nature.

A good scene often has tension that develops from one pole to the other.

### Character's Willpower

A weak-willed character cannot sustain conflict throughout a play, nor can they carry a drama.

### Plot vs. Character

Before constructing any situation, ask yourself three questions about the character:

What should I do?

What should others do?

What must be done?

Character is an element whose potential has not yet been discovered.

We only need conflict when it can prove the premise.

#### The Protagonist

The protagonist is the one who occupies the dominant position in any movement or causal relationship.

The protagonist must never be without ambition. They must desperately want something so badly that they would destroy everything — or bring about their own destruction — in pursuit of it.

A negative force, if sustained long enough, can become a positive force.

Characters become protagonists not because they wish to be, but because their inner needs and external circumstances compel them to take up the burden.

#### The Antagonist

The antagonist blocks the protagonist's progress, and the protagonist must use all their resources to oppose them.

In any play, the antagonist must be as strong and as determined as the protagonist.

From beginning to end, a drama is a crisis moving toward an inevitable conclusion.

### Character Orchestration

Orchestration means placing several clear and determined characters in opposition, allowing conflict to move the drama from one pole to another.

When watching a play, observe how each force is orchestrated. These forces can come from groups or individuals.

Every major shift contains many smaller shifts.

If you remain faithful to the three dimensions you have set for your characters, their words and actions will align with their nature — you do not need to artificially create differences between them.

If two characters happen to have conflict and are trying to prove the play's premise, the conflict will be richer and more colorful due to differences in expression. The dramatic effect of this contrast also depends on the characters' inner nature.

For any character, growth has countless possibilities. Without growth, the play loses the opposing attributes present at the beginning and hidden within the character's design. Lack of growth means no conflict, and no conflict means inadequate character orchestration.

### Unity of Opposites

In nature, nothing is "destroyed" or "dies" — everything is transformed into another form.

A true unity of opposites can only be broken when one or both sides completely change their traits or characteristics. In a true unity of opposites, it is impossible for either side to compromise.

After finding the premise, verify immediately whether the characters have this kind of unity of opposites. If their "unity" is not tight enough and their opposition not absolute enough, the conflict will appear flat, unable to escalate, and incapable of reaching a climax.
