I once read an article with a line that stuck with me:

> The work we do within eight hours defines our social role and professional standing... **but the eight hours outside of work determine who we can become.**

A day has 24 hours. Ideally, 8 for work, 8 for rest, and 8 for free time.

**That freely disposable 8 hours is where the gap between people truly lies.**

To make the most of those 8 hours, I've tried many things: learning knowledge from other industries, exchanging ideas with peers, cultivating new hobbies. But the most important of all is starting a **project that doesn't care about results**.

## What is a Project That Doesn't Care About Results?

- The word "project" is straightforward. _Getting Things Done_ nailed the definition — a series of planned actions aimed at a specific outcome. This implies a serious attitude and deliberate process.
- "Doesn't care about results" means you don't fixate on external rewards. The raw drive comes from genuine passion, not a thirst for praise. The starting point is pure interest.
- So, a project that doesn't care about results is something you're passionate about, and you plan it seriously.
- Isn't planning seriously at odds with following pure passion? Not at all. Following passion means no final destination; planning seriously means having concrete goals. For example, in a painting competition, I paint to win prize money and business opportunities — that's a purpose, and painting is merely a means to an end. A goal, on the other hand, is: I want to finish this painting before the deadline while meeting both my own standards and the contest's requirements.

## Why Take On a Project That Doesn't Care About Results?

There are several benefits:

- First, lasting motivation. When motivation comes from genuine interest — a purely intrinsic drive — it lasts far longer than money, favors, or recognition. You won't even feel like you're "struggling" to persist. I've always thought "striving hard" is a false concept when applied to passion projects. Working on them never feels like effort; it's more like playing a game. The moment you feel like you're struggling, it means you're encountering resistance. You think it's hard, and deep down you doubt you can do it. That resistance is entirely psychological — a sign that your interest in the thing isn't strong enough at the moment.
- Second, these projects produce _works_, not mere _commodities_. Commodities bring money, experience, and market opportunities — that's the kind of output the 8-hour workday is supposed to deliver. Continuing that outside work is just overtime. But works offer all that and more. Most importantly, a work reshapes you, guiding you toward the path that truly fits. This is the fundamental idea that choice matters more than effort.
- Third, astonishingly fast progress. After completing one or two of these projects — each taking anywhere from three months to a year — I've gained at least one or two extra skills. They may not meet market standards, but the leap from zero to sixty is so dramatic that it powerfully motivates you to find ways to get even better.

This website is one of my passion projects. Initially, I was interested in building my own site to showcase my case studies and CG artwork. But I gradually discovered that my interest in writing far exceeds my interest in coding and web design — and I have no intention of becoming a programmer professionally. So, keep it simple: just write. No purpose, just a goal: pick a topic I like each week and write about it. No word count requirements either. As for the structure, you've probably noticed every article has three sections:

- Main Course: My thoughts on the chosen topic.
- Creative Fragments: What I've been creating recently, and what I've learned.
- Weekly Highlight: Sharing something I find valuable.

The most famous project humanity has ever undertaken that didn't care about results was launching the [Voyager spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyager_program). This website is my own Voyager. What's yours?
