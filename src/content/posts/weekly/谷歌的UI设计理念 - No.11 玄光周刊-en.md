## [Google Design - Discover the people and stories behind the products](https://design.google/?home=)

![Google Design website showcasing Material Design philosophy, team stories, and product design case studies](../_images/11%20玄光周刊-Obsidian史诗级更新-1768401782243.webp)

I spent almost the entire last year dealing with UI/UX fields, saw many companies showcasing their unique UI design philosophy introductions. After circling around, it still comes back to Google 👍.

Google Design is Google's official platform showcasing its design philosophy, team stories, and thinking behind products. It's not just a portfolio but a window deeply exploring "how design shapes technology, and how technology serves people" [1].

Website content covers evolution from Material Design language to in-depth analysis of interaction details for specific products like Google Search, Maps, Assistant, etc. For designers and developers, this provides rare opportunities to understand how global top tech companies transform complex algorithms and massive data into accessible, intuitive experiences for ordinary people.

Content forms are very rich, including long-form design case studies, designer interviews, and thoughtful articles about design ethics and future trends. For example, you might read about challenges designing inclusive experiences for billions of global users, or how Google Fonts team develops and refines font systems supporting hundreds of languages. This content not only shows final design outcomes but reveals trade-offs behind decisions, failures and learning during iteration processes, and cross-functional team collaboration details.

## [The Motion Awards 2025](https://motionawards.com/)

![The Motion Awards 2025 website featuring global motion design winners, brand animation, and creative showcases](../_images/11%20玄光周刊-Obsidian史诗级更新-1768401964444.webp)

If we're talking about dynamic visuals, animation effects in UX fields still seem limited by inherent thinking constraints from my perspective. Looking across the entire dynamic visual field, I once gained infinite inspiration from The Motion Awards—striving to win this award once in my career. Dreams should exist.

The Motion Awards is an annual award focusing on recognizing outstanding achievements in global motion design. It covers motion graphics, brand animation, film title design, UI animation, advertising shorts, and other categories—an important stage for motion designers and studios to showcase strength and gain industry recognition. Participating works often represent the highest standards in creativity, technical execution, and narrative expression for that year's motion design. Browsing award-winning works is like watching a condensed annual design trend show, clearly seeing how new technologies (like real-time rendering, AI generation), new styles, and new narrative techniques are used by pioneering creators.

For practitioners or students, this heavyweight award's official website is a valuable learning resource and inspiration library.

You can systematically see how excellent motion design effectively conveys information and inspires emotions across different media and needs, from abstract visual experiments to commercial brand promotion. Many award-winning works include creator team interviews or production analyses, revealing complete processes from concept sketches to final films—involved software techniques, collaboration methods, and creative thinking are very inspiring.

## [Good design | About us | Vitsœ](https://www.vitsoe.com/eu/about/good-design)

![Vitsoe furniture brand Good Design page featuring Dieter Rams 606 Universal Shelving System and longevity philosophy](../_images/11%20玄光周刊-Obsidian史诗级更新-1768402843699.webp)

Closely related to UI design is naturally product design with display functions—but the product I want to talk about doesn't have one, which doesn't diminish how forward-thinking it is in the slightest.

Vitsœ is a furniture company famous for its "long-lasting design" philosophy. Its core product is the 606 Universal Shelving System designed by German design master Dieter Rams. On their "Good design" page, Vitsœ elaborates on their design philosophy of transcending trends and fashion. They believe good design should be durable, highly flexible, and use resources responsibly. This aligns with Dieter Rams' famous "Ten Principles of Good Design," emphasizing that aesthetic beauty must be built upon functional completeness and environmental friendliness.

Vitsœ doesn't just sell furniture—they're promoting a lifestyle that opposes blind consumption and pursues long-term value. Their shelving system was designed in 1960, still in production today, and fully compatible with old and new components—this itself is the most powerful interpretation of "sustainability." Reading their philosophy allows us, beyond digital product design, to gain profound reflection on product essence: should a product pursue fleeting visual impact and sales peaks, or should it be committed to becoming a long-lasting, reliable, and continually valuable part of users' lives? This thinking holds important reference significance for creators in any field when defining product value and design intent.

## [Why do our phones all use Heiti (sans-serif) instead of the prettier Kaiti?](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/fnG0l6Wa75nQ67k9QFPOVQ)

This article explores a phenomenon we take for granted in daily life but rarely examine deeply: why is the text you see on screens today, especially in Chinese, almost entirely dominated by Heiti (sans-serif), while the more aesthetically traditional Kaiti (regular script) and Songti (Song-style serif) fonts are rarely seen?

The article likely analyzes from multiple dimensions:

First is readability. Heiti has uniform stroke thickness and simple structure, with far higher recognition at small font sizes and low-resolution screens than Kaiti or Songti with their complex stroke details and serif decorations. Especially on mobile devices, where users need to quickly scan information, clear and unobstructed typography is crucial.

Second involves technical limitations and evolution. Early screens had low pixel density—in the bitmap font era, Heiti had the most stable performance. As screen technology developed, although display precision has greatly improved, Heiti's user perception as a "screen font" and design specifications have already been established.

Finally, there's unity of design logic: modern UI design pursues clear, neutral, efficient information transmission. Heiti's calm, characterless qualities perfectly fit this requirement—it serves as a "transparent container" for information, rather than carrying strong style and emotion like Kaiti, which could interfere with the content itself. Behind this lies the embodiment of functionalist design principles in font selection, a result of balancing practicality and aesthetics, tradition and digital media characteristics.
