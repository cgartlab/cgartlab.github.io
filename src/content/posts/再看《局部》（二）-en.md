In the end, I didn't finish Season 2 of "The Local." The on-site exhibition format focused more on the works themselves or the artists' biographies. I was more interested in the intriguing theories and perspectives.

Instead, a recommended [interview about IP and the Bauhaus](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_BB9sDlX3IM) on the side turned out to be much more inspiring.

## IP and Religion

> The most successful IP is religion.
>
> It has a self-consistent worldview, even supported by grand theoretical foundations.
> It has vivid characters and stories.

Religion provides a complete worldview and theoretical foundation, covering explanations of life, the universe, and human existence. This foundation typically involves a set of doctrines, scriptures, principles, and rituals — forming a solid framework for the religious IP.

These worldviews and theoretical foundations give the IP depth and breadth, allowing stories to explore and develop within the framework. At the same time, religion is filled with rich characters and stories that have become sources of inspiration. From ancient biblical figures to various mythological heroes, religious characters often represent morality, faith, or wisdom, carrying strong symbolic meaning.

These stories play a significant role not only in religious ceremonies and traditions but are also widely used in literature, art, and entertainment, becoming enduring IP resources.

## The Bauhaus

> The artist's predecessor is the craftsman.

Here's a story: a child's neighbor was an old shoemaker who spent his life making shoes by hand. A few years later, technology advanced, and factories could make shoes with machines. The child went back to visit the old man — the shop was gone, and so was he.

It's a simple, even dull story, but it's the plain truth playing out every day. The video talked about how the core idea of the Art Nouveau movement was to elevate the status of the craftsman and emphasize the importance of craftsmanship.

Then it moved on to the Bauhaus. The Bauhaus philosophy embraced the machine age, merging the artist and the craftsman. The result of merging these two identities turned out to be exactly my profession — the designer. But not the old kind of designer — this philosophy gave birth to a "new breed." They master the craftsman's skills while possessing the artist's aesthetic sensibilities.

The traditional view is that artists make beautiful but impractical things, while craftsmen make useful but unattractive things. The Bauhaus aimed to make objects both beautiful and useful — the more beautiful, the more useful; the more useful, the more beautiful.

I agree with the first part. But "the more beautiful, the more useful; the more useful, the more beautiful" — that's incredibly hard to achieve, a rare serendipity. Even if you pull it off, I believe the creator would feel it was the divine working through their hands, that they were merely a vessel.

> "Less is more" doesn't mean simplicity equals boredom. It means pouring more time and energy into the work, leaving no room for error.

Completely agree. But whether in my paintings or my writing, I'm far from the stage where "less" applies. I'm still climbing in "more and more." After all, if you haven't even reached "more," what's there to "less" about?
