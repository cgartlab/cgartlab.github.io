## Main Course This Week

Last week everything landed safely. In the first post, I mentioned how to deal with information overload — I knew I'd have to write about it sooner or later.

My method for handling information overload was learned piece by piece online, and by "piece by piece" I mean I took many detours. So far, the best tool I've found is building your own second brain.

## What Is a Second Brain?

> [!NOTE] This term isn't mine. I first saw it in Evernote's slogan. I don't use Evernote anymore, but I still believe in the concept of a second brain.

To understand what a second brain really means, we first need to understand the human brain. We must acknowledge a fact: the human brain is not good at rote memorization. Remember those "memorize and recite the full text" assignments from Chinese class? Think about how many poems you can still recite from back then. You'll notice that the ones you still remember are those where you can explain the meaning, the imagery, or the story behind them. The reason you can still recall them after all these years is not that you memorized the sequence of words — and note, it's not even that you understood their meaning at the time. The fundamental reason is that you produced thoughts around them (consciously or unconsciously). For example, the reason you remember "一览众山小" is probably not because you failed to write "会当凌绝顶" on an exam and got punished by writing it 20 times. It's more likely because you wanted to recite it perfectly at a reading event in front of that someone you secretly liked. What your brain actually did was link this poem to that person and that event — and that's why you'll probably never forget it. Thinking, after all, is about connecting seemingly unrelated things. So our brains are actually better at thinking; remembering knowledge is just a byproduct.

The essence of knowledge is useful information. Let's look at the memorization process. Every time we learn a new text, the first thing is to read it aloud — the brain is collecting information. Then the teacher explains the meaning of new words, the structure of paragraphs, the techniques the author used, the ideas expressed — the brain is organizing information, turning it into knowledge. Finally, exams come, and to get a good grade and avoid trouble at home, you need to recall this knowledge at the right moment — the brain is retrieving knowledge.

My second brain is a bionic system for collecting information, organizing information, and retrieving knowledge.

## Why Build a Second Brain?

Exams train us to solve problems.

When we enter society, we need to solve social problems. School exams are just competitive games with simple rules and a small scope. If our brain is a basket full of knowledge, then for this game, our built-in basket is basically sufficient.

Solving social problems, however, requires knowledge with a systematic structure. That knowledge is not only vast in volume but also complex and interconnected. Any problem at a professional level requires more than one or two university majors to handle comfortably, let alone a single online course. That's when you realize:

Our basket is too small. We need a bigger basket to hold knowledge. Our basket is too power-hungry — weighing 3% of our body, it consumes 20% of our daily energy, and then needs 8 hours of standby every day. Our basket is too unreliable — we lose important things like keys and IDs, and can't remember passwords unless they're a name plus birthday. All these problems are solved by using a second brain.

Now our brain can save a significant amount of energy for more efficient thinking, discovering connections between knowledge, and focusing more deeply on solving problems.

## How I Build My Second Brain

> [!tip] Since my background is in motion design and I work almost entirely on computers, from the moment I conceived of building a second brain, I wanted to use computers and mobile devices to make it happen.

Here are the tools and methods I currently use for reference:

For tools, I registered for Evernote out of curiosity back in 2011 and used it for 5 years. It stored thousands of articles, images, and links that I thought were "useful." Of course, 90% of those articles I've never opened again. I resisted upgrading to premium, so I only had 60MB of free sync traffic per month. Even so, after 5 years, my data took up about 2GB. Then I switched to OneNote, but the mobile editing and reading experience was unbearable. I also tried Youdao Cloud Notes, Apple Notes, and others — but that's beside the point.

In 2018, I signed up for Notion. Currently, Notion is the only software tool I use. What attracts me isn't the clean, beautiful UI, but the high degree of freedom and flexibility. I can build tools that truly meet my needs. For example, 90% of this website is powered by Notion, along with my business project management, task tracking system, and more. It basically meets all my needs for a second brain.

Of course, compared to what tool you use, what matters more is the method you use to use it. With the right method, the specific software doesn't matter. I've even seen someone pull this off with just a txt file. Here, I've set myself a principle: as simple as possible, but not simpler.

### Step 1: Collect Information

My information sources are mainly RSS feeds, newsletters, Telegram channels, books, and videos. Each has its pros and cons, but they share one thing in common: there's no algorithm constantly guessing my interests. Personally, I completely avoid any media platform that uses algorithmic recommendations for reading. I'd rather use a search engine. The end result of algorithmic recommendations is that you only see what they want you to see — you know what I mean. Algorithms can be used, but not be enslaved by them.

Collecting isn't just about saving "useful information." You should also record your own thoughts, even if it's just an annotation on a sentence. That way, future you will see that annotation and understand why you kept this article.

Note that at this stage, the information is still just information — it hasn't become knowledge yet.

> [!note] As for what to collect, from my experience, it's best to focus on a specific topic or project. For example, "how to build a second brain." Yes, that's the output stage. The reason will be explained later.

### Step 2: Organize and Review

Regularly clear your basket. If you're busy with work and your basket is always full of unread items, stop collecting. First, organize what you haven't processed yet, then continue. Trust me, slow is fast. Also, I believe organizing is not categorizing. It's not about slapping a bunch of tags on each piece of content and marking it as "read" — that's self-deception. The truly effective approach is to record your thoughts at that moment with a question in mind. For example, why am I highlighting this sentence? Is this knowledge point related to my work? How will it help me? Do I agree with the author's perspective? Do I have a similar case around me? This is what I mentioned earlier — let your brain do what it does best: think. At this point, information becomes knowledge.

Also, the purpose of organizing and reviewing is to build trust in yourself. After persisting for a while, you'll gradually get to know yourself psychologically and believe that what you've organized and reviewed meets your own standards. You might not end up loving the process, but your confidence will definitely grow.

> [!tip] Using Notion's Database feature is a great way to accomplish both collection and organization in one step.

### Step 3: Build Connections

Now we've finally arrived at the true stage of building a second brain. I actually do this step in parallel with organizing and reviewing — more accurately, it can be done anytime. At this stage, we begin to think deeply about the knowledge we've already come to know. My specific approach is to group similar articles into a topic, use a single note to consolidate the knowledge points scattered across several articles or videos, and then link the related content together. A few things to note:

- If there's no clear topic, start from wherever your interest takes you.
- You must summarize and express in your own words. This way, when you see it later, you'll understand it without having to read the original.
- Don't worry about being comprehensive. You can't get it perfect in one go. Take it slow and trust the process.
- Links should ideally be bidirectional — you can always come back.

At this point, knowledge begins to collide with your thoughts in奇妙 ways, and valuable ideas are hidden in these collisions. Building connections between knowledge is like creating portals in my knowledge base. Only then does the second brain truly come to life.

> [!tip] Using Notion's "Linked View of Database" feature is a great way to accomplish both collection and organization in one step.

### Step 4: Output Value

Investing in something must be for a return. In this step, it's about how to use the second brain.

Why go through all this trouble to absorb knowledge and build a second brain? On the surface, it's to better solve problems in life and work. But I believe the real purpose of acquiring knowledge is to enable us to output more value in the future. Value brings returns. Whether you're painting a picture, writing a journal entry, or completing a business project, you're essentially using your knowledge, making a series of decisions, taking a series of actions, and outputting your value to the outside world.

The common difficulty is that there's no visible return in the short term, because value growth follows an exponential curve. This leads to another management system called GTD, which I'm also reading about and practicing. I'll discuss it in a future article.

So, without using GTD, how do you make it easier to persist in building a second brain? My method is quite straightforward — I directly link the second brain to my commercial design projects, which are my livelihood. This way, input and output are naturally connected through a relationship that yields tangible returns.

At this point, while owning a second brain, a positive feedback loop forms within it.
