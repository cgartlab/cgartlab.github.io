I wasn't a good student back then. After a year of retaking the college entrance exam, I switched to studying art. Many of my classmates chose the art track too, signing up for all kinds of studios.

I hated homework as a kid. Summer vacation meant playtime. My parents worried I'd waste all my time, but I refused to go to tutoring. They wanted to find me a class to learn something else. Music, chess, calligraphy, painting — pick one. Kids love cartoons, so I picked the one that seemed closest to it. At heart, I just wanted to play.

Because I'd painted before, I thought I knew what painting was about. So when it came time for the college exam, choosing art was my own decision. I dared to make that choice. But my parents weren't so sure. Given my father's personality and his position as a schoolteacher, I suspect he looked down on art at the time. My mother didn't look down on it — she went to ask my old art teacher if this path was viable for me. Without hesitation, he said, come on in, any later and it'll be too late.

By the time I returned to the studio, I was a player with at least a five-year gap. A player with a gap — that's no fun anymore. Because the goal had become the exam, not pure enjoyment. The teacher was willing to take me in — I was lucky. He thought I had the stuff, that I could make a living from this, and he happened to have the energy to coach students through their final year.

While I was studying, an auntie — probably heard from my mother that I was doing well and the teacher was good — asked my mom if her child could pursue the same path. The kid came and painted with me for a few days. The teacher sent him home. He told the auntie to find another path for her child — this wasn't for him. Thinking back, I have immense respect for that teacher. He gave a firm red light. But a red light is still a guiding light. It points to a dead end. A dead end is still a road — one you simply don't take. I could see the kid was crushed, but I also knew his luck was as good as mine.

Later, when I started working, I ended up training students at various institutions and companies, teaching basic animation techniques. I met plenty of students who weren't cut out for it. But the institution wasn't mine, so I had no say in whether they stayed. What does a teacher do when faced with a student who isn't meant for this? Of course, many teachers can't even tell if a student has what it takes — they can't even tell if they themselves do. A student does poorly, so you point out what's wrong here and there, tell them how to improve. Specific, detailed corrections — I can list a dozen issues. You nod, agree, follow the advice. But the final result is still the same — you still can't do it. Maybe you'll succeed this once, but you can't make a living from it.

You're Guo Degang trying to imitate Tony Leung — it just doesn't work. They're not the same type. Tony Leung can't imitate Guo Degang either. Correct details may hide a larger error — a macro error, a wrong direction. If Guo Degang actually tried to learn from Tony Leung, a director would point out every mismatch, listing them one by one. But what he really should be told is that he shouldn't be imitating anyone at all. A qualified teacher's first job is to tell the student this. Right away: you can't make a living from this, this path is a dead end, money won't help. If you only point out specific mistakes, you let the student keep believing they have what it takes — and that's misleading.

Spending money on a class, taking an online course, learning a skill — only to discover that your biggest takeaway is that you're not suited for this. Is it worth it? Many people think not. I've spent money and time, not only failed to learn, but got a bucket of cold water from the teacher. The teacher showed a red light, and they think he's a terrible teacher.

But students should first ask themselves: am I cut out for this? Anyone who's thought about this question probably wouldn't even sign up for the class. If they did, they'd figure out in two sessions that it's not for them and wouldn't renew. Of course, most teachers would rather they did renew.

Students who never ask themselves this question — even after they've hit a wall and are battered and bruised — still think: maybe the timing was bad, maybe I didn't work hard enough, maybe the teacher wasn't good enough. They only accept external reasons. They won't accept the internal one: that they simply aren't suited for this, that no amount of effort will make it work.

So when you don't have the luck to meet a mentor who can guide you, what's truly valuable is self-awareness. Without seeing the truth, gaining that self-awareness on your own comes at a great cost. But that same self-awareness will save you from paying an even greater one.
