## Introduction

This series started as a small booklet I planned to write for my parents. This first chapter is also my attempt to see if I can handle long-form writing at a larger scale.

The reason is simple: over the past two years, after my enthusiastic evangelizing, my parents have gotten used to using various AI products. Now they use AI daily — from looking up recipes to understanding basic legal questions, from setting World Cup match reminders to tinkering with their own content creation. Almost everything.

What's interesting is that while they didn't grow up in the mobile internet era like younger generations, they've been using smartphones for over a decade. Yet no matter how smart phones get, even with AI as a convenient "black tech" assistant, these patterns keep appearing:

```markdown
- Staring at a blank input box with nothing to say
- Going from "never think to ask AI" to "wanting to ask AI about everything"
- Finally knowing what you want to ask, but not knowing how to phrase it
- Giving up after one unsatisfactory answer, never trying again
- Getting a great answer, then losing it two days later and having to ask again
- Every conversation is "one-off" — starting from zero next time
- Can't tell if AI is confidently spewing nonsense
- Blindly sharing personal information
- Clicking "Agree" or "OK" on popups without a second thought
- ······
```

Now look at us so-called "young people." We grew up in the smartphone era, seemingly born knowing how to use dozens of apps, born knowing how to tell which information is more reliable. But when it comes to the rapidly evolving age of AI, we're no different from our parents facing smartphones for the first time. Because every single issue above applied to me just a few years ago.

Even though most people who know me consider me a [Geek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geek), I've often felt helpless in the face of AI platforms and products changing at breakneck speed. I just recognized this as an incredibly powerful tool and refused to let it slip by. Then I picked up writing again in recent years, and accumulated a lot of "handy tips" that genuinely improved my workflow.

So I decided to document and share how I solved these problems — to distill years of accumulated know-how into something systematic and worth passing on. I believe it's work with long-term value.

Before you decide to read on, a few things to know:

- This series is for beginners. If you're already experienced, you probably don't need this.
- This series is completely free. Commercial use is prohibited. Feel free to share with attribution.
- The outline and first draft were written by me, with refinement and suggestions from Codex.
- All AI use cases in this series use the ChatGPT web version, with links provided.
- All images in this series are AI-generated.
- This is **not** a quick-start guide. If you're looking for shortcuts, you can stop now.
- This series will take you from a "rule executor" to a "rule designer" — someone who can measurably improve AI output quality.

## Why Not a Quick-Start Guide?

![Why Not a Quick-Start Guide](./_images/1-慢一点比较快-1785423604478.webp)

Since the 1990s, computers and the internet have accelerated the world's pace by an order of magnitude. People keep demanding faster information, faster growth, faster returns.

Faster, cheaper, better — it's an eternal human need.

And AI? By every measure — content generation speed, pace of development, capital inflow, resource consumption — it's the fastest industry there is, bar none.

You might wonder: what does this have to do with using AI well? Everything. Because AI is already fast, and we built it to help us go even faster.

So what's the rush? When it comes to actually *using* this tool well, I think rushing is exactly the wrong thing to do.

If there's only one reason, it's this: **AI is an amplifier.** Everything you have — strengths and weaknesses alike — gets amplified. Faced with this revolutionary amplifier, you should be more cautious, not less, and learn to use it systematically.

If there are multiple reasons, they look like this:

```text
- Learning can be accelerated, but there are no shortcuts
- The quality of your questions determines the quality of your answers
- Good habits and thinking methods take time to develop
- Habits and mindsets can't be built in a day
- Tools become obsolete; mindsets and habits don't
```

Notice something? None of these reasons have anything to do with AI itself. AI doesn't care how well or poorly you use it. It just mercilessly, relentlessly, and ever-faster amplifies whatever you already have.

Amplifying strengths makes sense. But how do you amplify weaknesses? Simple: if others' strengths get amplified and yours don't, that's effectively your weakness being amplified too, isn't it?

So the question becomes: how do I grow my strengths and shrink my weaknesses? How do I get AI to amplify more of the good and less of the bad?

In other words, what I really need to overcome are some deceptively simple, age-old questions:

> How do you learn and think about unfamiliar domains?

That's the real skill this entire series is trying to pass on to you. And it's applicable to any skill you want to learn.

### Text Is Already the Fastest Information Medium

There's a clue in how ChatGPT emerged. All large language models were first trained on massive amounts of text before they could generate images or video. Ever wonder why that order?

From a pure information storage and transmission perspective: a photo taken on a phone averages about 2MB. The same file size in text can hold hundreds of thousands of characters. In other words, **text is the most efficient information carrier in the digital world, period.**

But the human brain is wired for images and sound — which is why short-form video is so explosively popular. It's even spawned the famous [TL;DR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TL;DR) phenomenon. When you think about it, that's a pretty curious state of affairs.

> AI is faithfully reading every single word, while humans can't wait to get a 200-word summary and call it a day.

This article is already several thousand words long. If you've made it this far, you're already ahead of most people. The further you read, the more you'll realize how important patient reading really is.

### Identifying Long-Term Value

![](./_images/1-慢一点比较快-1785423198130.webp)

Slowing down and staying patient serves another critical purpose: identifying long-term value. In my experience, this is the foundation of virtually every important decision.

Director Guo Fan often says on set:

> Don't rush. Slow is fast.

Of course, they can't actually afford to be slow on a film set. But I deeply agree with the philosophy.

Real learning has no shortcuts. Even GPT took seven years from R&D to launch. As long as they don't drop the ball, that gap is incredibly hard for competitors to close — they'd have to find a clever workaround.

Slow almost always means long-term. And we all agree that doing valuable things is the right thing. In other words, long-term value means persisting in doing the right thing.

Identifying long-term value comes down to one question:

> Are you doing the right thing, or are you doing things right?

This is one reason I chose to write this booklet. Learning techniques matters, but in an AI landscape that changes this fast, most techniques become obsolete. They have no long-term value.

Things with long-term value tend to share these traits:

- **General enough** — once you learn it, you can apply it across domains.
- **Stable enough** — it has persisted through civilization and won't be easily淘汰.
- **Simple enough** — like the laws of nature themselves.

If you've made it this far, you probably already know who in your circle is working on things with long-term value.

So what's the single biggest long-term value for ordinary people like us when it comes to using AI?

**Learning how to design rules.**

## From Rule Executor to Rule Designer

![From Rule Executor to Rule Designer](./_images/1-慢一点比较快-1785423129207.webp)

Remember the patterns from the beginning?

```markdown
- Staring at a blank input box with nothing to say
- Going from "never think to ask AI" to "wanting to ask AI about everything"
- Finally knowing what you want to ask, but not knowing how to phrase it
```

At first, I thought the inability to ask good questions was a language and expression problem. But I quickly realized that couldn't be right — AI is *great* at organizing thoughts and polishing language. So why was I still getting mediocre answers?

Then it clicked. It's the inertia of being an employee. I'm a designer by trade — design is fundamentally about solving problems. Most jobs are the same. But now the roles are reversed: I want AI to solve *my* problem, which means I suddenly have to be the one who defines the problem and the requirements.

And defining good problems and requirements is essentially **designing rules**.

At work — and actually, not just at work — during our waking hours, most of us are rule executors. Wake up by the alarm, shower, eat, commute, work, overtime, go home, sleep. Most people never stop to ask whether the rules they follow every day are even correct, let alone how to design better ones.

And when something feels off? The most common thought is: "Great, finally done following rules for today. Time to relax." And then you seamlessly switch into *another* set of rules...

The first real step to using AI well is learning to design rules yourself.

Who do you learn from? The people who are good at designing rules, of course. But you quickly realize that the people who design rules in life are usually bosses, clients, leaders...

The good news is, AI is also great at designing rules.

Asking AI a high-quality question *is* a miniature act of rule design. We'll explore how to systematically design these "rules" in Chapter 3.

### Finding the Best Rule Designer

AI is easily the most powerful rule designer that ordinary people can access and afford. Another thing that's obvious: the more expensive, the better.

In 2026, among the working professionals and students I've talked to, many still think paying for a chatbot — especially a monthly subscription — is completely unreasonable:

- The most common reason I hear from office workers: "Work tools should be paid for by the company."
- The most common reason from students: "Free is good enough."

Both perspectives are understandable, but they're still coming from the mindset of a rule executor. They've never thought about becoming a rule designer.

The people who are good at designing rules in everyday life are usually clients, managers, school administrators, teachers, politicians — in short, the "few smart people."

Think about it: if you went to the best boss, manager, or client you know and asked them to teach you how to design rules, how much would it cost to get a genuinely useful answer?

Now, for less than 200 yuan a month, you can hire a 24/7 strategy expert who never complains and can answer virtually any question you have. Is that a good deal? If it is, why wouldn't you pick the best one?

Once you realize this, you'll probably agree with these sincere suggestions:

- Your time is valuable.
- Within your means, choose the most advanced AI product and service that is widely recognized as such.
- Preferably the one the capital market also believes is the most advanced.
- If it takes a bit of hassle to set up, don't be afraid of the trouble.

These sound like a bunch of "obvious truths" — but precisely because they're so obvious, most people overlook them.

I should also acknowledge that unlike previous technological revolutions (computers, the internet, smartphones) which seemed to democratize access, the reality with AI is different. The most advanced models often require a significant subscription, and those willing to pay for them are still a minority.

By now, I trust you've chosen the platform and product that suits you, or are ready to get to know AI as a master of rule design. From here on, I'll use the ChatGPT web version for all examples. (If you use DeepSeek, Kimi, Doubao, or any other platform, everything still applies.)

### Why Start with the Web Version?

![Why Start with the Web Version](./_images/1-慢一点比较快-1785423775028.webp)

Whether it's ChatGPT, Claude, DeepSeek, Kimi, or GLM, mature AI products typically run in six modes:

- Web
- Mobile app
- Desktop app
- CLI
- API
- Agent

Most people only encounter the first three. So why do I insist on using the ChatGPT web version for all examples? I actually asked ChatGPT this question. Here's part of its answer:

> **If I were to recommend a learning path for regular users**
> Most people don't need to study every entry point from the start.
> A reasonable path would be:
>
> **Phase 1: Web**
> Understand the basic ChatGPT workflow:
> "Ask a question → provide context → let AI generate → refine."
> This is the most important step.
>
> **Phase 2: Mobile App**
> Turn ChatGPT into a daily tool.
> For example:
> - Take a photo of a menu and have AI translate it
> - Take a photo of a problem you can't solve and have AI explain it
> - Use voice chat while walking
> - Jot down ideas on the go
>
> **Phase 3: Desktop**
> When ChatGPT starts being part of your work, the desktop version becomes much more valuable.
> For designers:
> - Upload a design draft and have AI analyze it
> - Discuss solutions
> - Read project files
> - Help with copywriting
> For programmers:
> - Review code
> - Modify files
> - Debug issues
> The desktop app has now deeply integrated with local files, browsers, and development tools
>
> [Original conversation link](https://chatgpt.com/share/6a5394a8-df34-83ee-a3f1-efc39e5d0b8c)

After getting that answer, I also considered my own experience:

When I first started using AI, the mobile app was the first thing I ruled out.

- **First, small screens are not suitable for productive work**[^1].
  - If someone can do their entire job on a phone, they're probably not doing the kind of work that requires deep thinking and complex creation.
  - For high-complexity information processing, creation, and management tasks, a computer remains the most productive personal device.
- **Second, it's easy to get distracted.**
  - A phone has dozens of other apps. A call or message can break your train of thought instantly.
  - Switching to entertainment mode is one tap away — need I say more?
- **Third, staring at a phone screen damages your eyesight faster. Moving on.**

At the end of the day, I see the phone as a device for communication and entertainment consumption. It's for *maintaining* production, not *engaging* in it. Also, on phones, there's both a web version and an app version. For ChatGPT, I still recommend the web version because installing apps from international app stores is genuinely inconvenient, regardless of your phone's OS. You can use the "Add to Home Screen" feature in your mobile browser to create a shortcut.

This naturally leads to the web version's core advantages:

- **Works almost everywhere.**
  - Any device with an internet connection and a decent browser can use it.
- **Data and features update instantly.**
  - Data sync is obvious.
  - For features: apps require downloading new versions; the web always has the latest.
  - Platform announcements and changes are usually published on the website first.
- **Lower learning curve.**
  - This might be counterintuitive, so let me explain. The web version and clients (desktop + mobile apps) share one thing: almost all advanced or pro features are in that "Settings" panel.
  - But finding an advanced feature in the web version usually takes fewer steps than in the app. Apps bury advanced features deeper.
  - Don't underestimate that extra click. One more step, and click-through rates drop off a cliff.

I have to admit that in 2026, the web version is no longer as dominant as it once was:

- 83% of global AI usage happens on mobile apps (Graphite.io, March 2026)
- ChatGPT is the fastest app ever to reach 1 billion monthly active users (Sensor Tower, May 2026)
- ChatGPT's web traffic share dropped from 76% to 53%; Gemini now holds 27-28% (Similarweb, May 2026)

My recommendation to start with the web version is purely my personal opinion. If you're already comfortable with the app or desktop client, the methods discussed later will work just as well.

## The Patience to Read Text

After more than a decade working in visual creative fields, I've come to a firm conclusion: the ability to patiently read a block of text has never been more important than it is today.

![The Patience to Read Text](./_images/1-慢一点比较快-1785423099160.webp)

### Images and Sound Are Weakening Reading Ability

Here's a counterintuitive fact: in the digital age, people are actually reading *more* than ever.

I studied animation in college, and for years I believed that images and video carried more information than text. It seemed obvious on a computer: a high-definition movie is at least 10GB, while all seven Harry Potter books are barely 10MB. So I naively thought text was trivial.

The big misunderstanding is this: **file size ≠ information density.** When it comes to the information density your brain actually processes, **text and images carry fundamentally different types of information** — images excel at recording concrete, sensory, spatial information; text excels at abstract, logical, structured information.

An image contains fixed visual elements: color, light, space, subject, environment. Notice that unless you're the creator, every image is *fixed*. Video is the same, just adding a time dimension — a sequence of images played in order. Still *fixed*.

But text is different. A single sentence carries information beyond sight and sound — smell, taste, even the rhetorical device of synesthesia where you "see sweetness." Compared to images, text doesn't convey fixed information.

If you agree with this, it's not hard to conclude: **images are good at recording the world; text is good at constructing worlds.**

In an age flooded with visual media, most people consume mostly images most of the time. The inevitable result is that they grow increasingly impatient with reading text. They're gradually losing the ability to read with patience.

The flip side: it's actually getting easier to surpass most people. You just need "a little more patience to read a few more lines."

### AI Evolved Through Text First

From the moment ChatGPT appeared, this fact was on the table: all large language models are trained by reading massive amounts of text *before* they can generate images or video.

I once wrote an article on CGArtLab called *Fragmented Writing: Building a Thinking Specimen*, where I used an analogy:

> **"Writing is also a form of programming. Articles and books are like programs that the human brain can run."**

This means: the words you read and write aren't just "information data" — they're "code." They should be structured, reusable, and connectable. And AI is the tool best suited to processing this kind of "code."

So when you use AI, you're actually having a conversation with an intelligence that *evolved through text*. It understands text more purely, comprehensively, and thoroughly than most humans do.

Remember this line from earlier?

> AI is faithfully reading every single word, while humans can't wait to get a 200-word summary and call it a day.

**When it comes to collaborating with AI, text is the most efficient interaction medium.** Because AI itself is trained on text, your ability to express needs in words directly determines the quality of AI's output.

Most people want to grab the conclusion and run, unwilling to spend an extra second reading. The flip side: it's getting easier to surpass most people. Just be willing to read a little more and a little longer.

### But It Can Confidently Hallucinate

Because AI learns through text, it also inherits text's errors, biases, and contradictions. When facing AI output, patient reading serves two purposes: getting the most complete, high-density information, and building a critical line of defense against hallucination.

Li Xiaolai wrote in *You Must Use Computers Well*:

> If you're willing to read the manual as soon as you get any tool, you've already beaten 99% of people.

99% of computer and software error messages are in English. In the past, English was a real barrier for many. But ChatGPT has completely eliminated that barrier — you can throw any error message at it, and it will take you from the problem straight to the answer.

But there's a key prerequisite: you have to have the patience to read. The root of poor reading comprehension is a lack of patience. Always wanting to go faster, to take shortcuts, to get the result directly. The most common phrase in these people's minds and mouths is: "I don't want to hear/read/know all that. Just tell me... how to do it!"

I know this from personal experience. I used to be the same way — when faced with a problem, my first instinct was "is there an existing answer?" instead of "let me look at the instructions first." That habit cost me countless valuable pieces of information, and it also made me more susceptible to being misled by AI's confident nonsense in the early days.

Here are the domains where AI still has a high probability of hallucination, compiled with GPT's help, for reference:

> - Law
> - Medicine
> - Current events, people
> - Academic references
>
> [Original conversation link](https://chatgpt.com/share/6a6d74fa-84b8-83ea-bf90-ee5851b34c08)

Most people are used to taking things at face value. They get a conclusion and use it without a second thought. The flip side: to surpass most people, you just need to "check it one more time."

### Official Documentation Is a Good Thing

Think of official documentation as a software's "user manual." It's often called "Help," "Guide," "More," or sometimes a circled question mark, like this:

![Help icon](./_images/1-慢一点比较快-1785419311709.webp)

You might wonder: why should I read official documentation when I can just ask AI?

In the age of AI, official documentation is actually *more* valuable. Here's why:

**First, documentation is the source of rules.** AI can help interpret documentation, but the documentation itself is the authority. When AI gives an answer, cross-referencing it with official documentation is the best way to avoid being misled by confident nonsense. (Of course, if you're skilled with Agents, this problem can be mitigated — but that's a topic for later.)

**Second, documentation is a vessel for long-term value.** Techniques become obsolete, but the underlying logic and rule-design principles embedded in documentation often have lasting value. As I said: "Tools become obsolete; mindsets and habits don't."

**Third, reading documentation is itself training.** Every time you patiently read a document, you're exercising your deep reading ability. This skill applies not just to AI, but to any scenario that requires understanding a complex system. Most documentation is now web-based, searchable by keyword, and much less of a chore than you'd imagine.

**Fourth, when you learn to verify AI through official documentation, you're practicing a fundamental form of data sovereignty — not blindly trusting a single source.** In the next chapter, we'll extend this awareness to an even more fundamental level: how to ensure your data itself is secure and under your control.

In my personal knowledge management system, I have a principle: **every important framework design decision should be backed by corresponding theoretical documentation.** Not because I trust the documentation, but because I trust *traceability* — when AI gives a suggestion, I can quickly trace back to the original documentation and verify its soundness.

## Summary

I used to dismiss these so-called "fundamental understandings" too. I thought I already "knew" them. Then reality slapped me back to square one, again and again, until I finally accepted that there are no shortcuts. If you've made it this far and still think "OK, I already know this," let me share a quote that hit me hard:

> You know that a hot kettle will burn you. No, you don't. You only *know* when you've actually touched it, felt what "hot" means, and will never touch it again.

The other point is this: that initial feeling of helplessness with AI — having nothing to say, not knowing where to start, getting answers you can't use — the root cause isn't that AI is hard. It's that we've spent our entire lives *executing* rules, but never deliberately learned or practiced *designing* them.

AI is a ruthless amplifier. It doesn't distinguish between good and bad. It just amplifies whatever you already have — strengths and weaknesses alike. If you want it to amplify your strengths rather than your weaknesses, the only path is to slow down and systematically rebuild your relationship with information, with tools, and with thinking itself.

Before you learn how to use AI well, there are three foundational understandings to establish:

1. **Identify long-term value.** In an age obsessed with "faster, cheaper, better," you should be *more* cautious, not less. Things with long-term value are usually general enough, stable enough, and simple enough — like the skill of "learning to design rules."

2. **Shift your identity.** From rule executor to rule designer. AI is the most powerful and affordable mentor for rule design — but only if you're willing to invest in it, whether in money or time.

3. **Rebuild reading ability.** In an age of visual overload, patient reading is becoming a scarce competitive advantage. AI evolved through text, and text is the most efficient medium for collaborating with it. But that also means you need a verification mechanism — official documentation is your last line of defense against AI hallucination.

Don't be afraid to go slow. Slowing down is how you find your direction. In the next chapter, we'll carry this patience forward and learn how to design our first rule: from data security to prompt engineering, building your own AI rule system step by step.
