Recently on X, I saw plenty of posts from developers and designers — many of them young or fresh out of school — talking about quitting or job-hunting. Work is hard to find, and some big accounts even tag posts "#Hiring" to help spread recruiting info. A friend of mine has had a rough time switching jobs too, and a while back he sent me his portfolio to look over.

With that as a prompt, I sorted through the question and thought I'd share some experience from my angle, hoping it helps more people who are job-hunting.

I currently run my own studio, so I look at this more from the interviewer's side: how do I judge whether someone is worth hiring? This piece is me laying out what I'm actually looking at and thinking when I sit across the table and flip through a designer's résumé or portfolio.

Let me start with a premise: **right now, a designer's portfolio matters far more than their résumé or interview patter.** When I, as an employer, judge a person, I don't much care what they've done in the past — the past is easy to inflate, even to fake, and it can't show the real level of the person in front of me. At most it reflects their attitude toward the quality of their own output.

What an employer cares about more: whether, through a portfolio, a first impression, an interview, I can judge if this person can hold up when dropped into my production process.

From that angle, old-school portfolios — documents, slide decks, even video — can't answer that question.

And now that designers have AI at their disposal, I think this problem is actually solvable — but first you have to rethink what a portfolio is. I'm a case in point: over the past few years, from learning to build websites and picking up the underlying knowledge of the internet, to coding and then developing my own tools, I've learned almost all of it by asking AI along the way.

### Your Portfolio Can't Be a Play-by-Play

My friend's portfolio, honestly, wasn't bad. From a graphic-design standpoint his ability was clearly there. Clean layout, enough experience and volume, laid out one after another, packed full.

But after flipping through it I told him: as an interviewer, I'm actually not reassured. I know he's done a lot of projects — some of them we did together — but knowing he's capable doesn't help; a document just can't convince anyone. Many of the cases were from a year or two ago, or even earlier. If the him of today took on new design styles and trends, would he be better than back then? By how much? When he hits a new requirement he's never seen, how does he break it down, what does he trade off? These are questions none of those dozens of documents can answer.

It's not just him. I've seen too many documents like this over the years — essentially an appendix to a résumé, proving you "were in this fight" or "were here," but not proving "where you are now." And that's exactly what an employer wants. When I hire someone, I'm betting him on my next project; I need to know where his ceiling is right now, not merely how many trophies he collected in the past.

### How I Built My Own Portfolio

My own portfolio has changed form three times. At first, of course, it was for job-hunting; later it was more for showing clients and pitching projects — sounds different from an interview, right? But I think it's essentially the same thing: both are about selling your value and turning it into money.

When I graduated, like every undergrad, I had no experience — just a few assignments and project stints. That's how it is at the starter village.

If you're at that stage, what matters more is **being open to advice and being reliable — a good mentor will naturally be willing to help you.**

Later, through work, my own freelance gigs, and studying abroad, I accumulated enough work, so I made a showreel video. And when I was job-hunting again, I found a video worked far better than a document.

But a video is still static in essence. Once it's done it's frozen; I could only remake it every few years — one version for 2016–2020, another for 2020–2024.

Static things have a flaw: the moment it's finished, it starts going stale. After that, no matter how much my thinking evolves or how many rounds the tech iterates, it's hard to reflect that in a medium that doesn't move.

It took me a long time to figure out: **what matters enormously is whether the carrier can be updated at any time, whatever the carrier is** — a carrier that can prove your problem-solving ability to the fullest, at any moment. Documents, slides, even video won't do; they're all "printed things." A video at best carries a bit of interaction — click pause, fast-forward, rewind.

So, back in 2022, when AI could only chat, I resolved to learn how to build websites. Starting from the simplest HTML and PHP, to WordPress, to how to buy a domain, configure DNS, accelerate with a CDN, what GitHub is, what Cloudflare is… I chewed through all this underlying web knowledge one by one — much of it by asking AI — until my first site went live.

![cgartlab.com, founded in 2022, archived by the Internet Archive two years later](../_images/设计师工作难找，不要甩锅给大环境%20-%20No.26-1794741723175.webp)

In hindsight, learning to build a website was itself a real exercise in "find a problem, solve it, keep iterating." The website you see is itself a work I iterated — design, content, architecture, interaction all keep changing; the point is, it's alive, it breathes.

### The Extra Dimensions a Website Has

A living website and a document or a video differ by far more than "whether it moves" — they differ in **dimensions**.

Setting pure technical concerns aside and looking only at design, let's run through how many things you have to handle at once to do a website well: basics like theme, color, composition, typography, layout; above that UI component design, interaction design, transitions/animation, video, even 3D models.

Each of these, in college, is enough for a whole course of its own. When you open a website someone made, these seven or eight dimensions hit you all at once; a document has only layout and graphics, a video only picture, sound and a timeline. The dimensional gap is an order of magnitude, the information an interviewer can receive is an order of magnitude different, and your real ability gets discounted in their eyes.

I wrote about this back in [Issue No.20](https://cgartlab.com/posts/weekly-20/): I've always felt a designer should have an online portfolio they designed themselves — a blog counts too. For introducing yourself, for doing business, sending a link beats sending dozens of PDF pages by a mile.

Beyond that, a website has two more special dimensions.

**First, besides showing your design skill and taste, it also lets people feel your writing and expression ability.** With a document or video, that only comes across in a one-on-one interview; a website does it without even meeting.

**Second, it lets people feel your learning speed more directly.**

Over this past year of learning AI, I tried rebuilding my own [online portfolio](https://cy92.org) outside the blog. I put the early work in first, and once the features and look were stable, added new pieces bit by bit. The blog portfolio works the same way — [2016–2020](https://cgartlab.com/posts/demo-2020/) one version, [2020–2024](https://cgartlab.com/posts/demo-2024/) another, and barring surprises, a new version around 2028.

![cy92.org](../_images/设计师工作难找，是%20AI%20太强了吗%20-%20No.26-1794753472944.webp)

When an interviewer looks at a live website, they see not only how strong the candidate is now, but **how fast they're getting stronger.** That's information even a conversation can't give. Conversely, a site that keeps updating is itself telling them: I'm far from my ceiling.

### Made for Others vs. Made for Yourself

This is the layer I think is most often overlooked, and yet most crucial.

The projects you stuff into a portfolio are, the vast majority, done for clients. Doing work for a client means there's a requester, a client, all kinds of compromise; what comes out is "something that satisfies someone else." However good, it's still the past, it's what someone else wanted — not exactly what the employer in front of you wants.

But **the website itself is a work you make for yourself.** Its creative freedom is maxed out; you're only accountable to your own judgment. What goes on it, how it's arranged, how finely each detail is polished — nobody forces you, it's all your call. So what a website reveals is precisely the part of your taste and judgment **you refused to compromise in commercial projects.** That layer is something you'll never see in a commercial project, because there it's only the client-approved version.

That said, all of the above holds under one precondition — you have to stop locking yourself into the "designer" identity.

### Forget Your Major

Some might think: easy for you to say — you're already the boss, so you can talk without your back aching. Designers will be wiped out by AI sooner or later; the models can now build 3D, write code, even output video and games directly. Surely the future doesn't need this many designers.

True, I'll admit it: very soon most designers really will be wiped out.

**But my view is: who ever said design work must be done by "designers"?**

Looking at it that way is still student thinking — taking the major you studied and boxing your thinking into a fixed industry. But the real business world absolutely does not map one-to-one to college majors; the end goal of knowledge and skill is always to put it to use.

The design industry today, accelerated by AI, will most likely need stronger "hexagon warriors." Because AI plus a student or novice designer who knows how to use it — in an employer's eyes that's already a junior hexagon warrior.

**I can tell the world I'm a designer, but I'll never take it seriously and just bury my head in design.**

If you're a student, forget your major — a major exists only for that one moment of getting into college; after you're in, it basically doesn't exist.

If you're a newcomer, forget that you're a designer — with or without AI, the real jungle world is only ever selection and exchange.

### In Summary

![Source: Unsplash](../_images/工作难找，不要甩锅给大环境%20-%20No.26-判断.jpg)

On the market there's an employer A and an employee B.

Question 1: Why on earth would A be willing to spend real money to hire another B he's never met to work together?

The answer: A **believes** B can solve a problem A faces or might face. Note that **believing** is enough.

Question 2: How does B get an A he's never met to spend real money to bring B along?

The answer: find a way to make A **believe** B can solve their problem — and then **actually solve it.**

And what we call "the bad environment" is essentially complaining that there are fewer A's and more B's on the market.

This sounds like correct nonsense, right? But most B's who've gone through interview rejections or layoffs tend not to think about this — they just blame there being fewer A's.

The real problem for B is: either they failed to make the next A hold that belief, or they got cut because they couldn't actually solve the previous A's problem — and that has little to do with how many A's and B's there are.

### Redesigned the Blog Homepage

![cgartlab.com](../_images/设计师工作难找，是%20AI%20太强了吗%20-%20No.26-1794753610887.webp)

A while ago I redid the blog homepage entirely; it went live at the end of October. The old one was just a list of posts from the very start, and a friend kept telling me to make a proper homepage — otherwise it doesn't look like a motion designer's site.

My blog is positioned as a container for my personal digital content. I referenced the Braun radio style and made it a box. Each nav section uses a small mechanical device to summarize its character — a mix of cyber and retro, which I really like. On desktop, hovering switches it; on mobile where there's no hover, it loops by default. The whole thing took about 10 hours, done by [OpenCode](https://opencode.ai/) and [DeepSeek](https://www.deepseek.com/) 4.1 Flash; it burned through nearly 2 billion tokens.

And less than a month later, I'd already retired [OpenCode](https://opencode.ai) — again marveling at the speed of tech iteration.

### Rebuilding the Vault's Tags: From 132 Down to 63

No matter what knowledge-management tool I use, the thing I least want to touch is tags — I used to tag carelessly and it became a mess; for me a tag system collapses within two days. This collapse has history: I wrote about a round of it in [*Anatomy of a Designer's Second Brain*](https://cgartlab.com/posts/second-brain-for-designer/). From Evernote to OneNote to Notion and Obsidian, I switched tools several times and never got tags sorted out.

But with AI it's very convenient to manage them. After a few discussions I realized I just hadn't fully understood how to use tags — the right way is to treat them as Weibo topics. (Personal view.)

I talked it over with AI and settled on three layers:

Layer one, the skeleton, still starts from the basic directory structure — this is for AI to see the hierarchy clearly.

Layer two is each note's single theme, extended by 2–3 tags. For example, studying a person's profile naturally creates tags for their company, product, or work.

Layer three is cross-domain — say a note goes from art to philosophy to content creation; then I add another 2–3 tags, no more. Tags must be strictly limited in number, otherwise searching turns up unrelated noise.

Honestly this is already too complex for me — I don't have the energy to maintain it. Building another tag system on top of hundreds of notes is work tailored for AI. The essence is doing a subtraction, so the computation behind the scenes stays light.

## Recommended Tools

### [Tack-Tile: A Digital Archive of Paper Goods, 1780–1980](https://www.tack-tile.com/)

![1895 newspaper and periodical stamp archive page — historical paper goods and type specimens](../_images/工作难找，不要甩锅给大环境%20-%20No.26-TackTile.jpg)

It collects packaging, posters, tickets, matchboxes, coasters, receipts, ads, catalogs, type specimens, sign-painter manuals, calligraphy guides, magazines, fairy tales, encyclopedias, dictionaries, recipes, and more — newly collected, scanned, and archived every month. Collected by Mitch Wiesen, written by Hala Maher, site built by Evan Whitaker; membership-based, with free samples, some images licensable for commercial use.

I put it in this issue's recommendations because it makes one thing explicit: what's valuable in a portfolio is likewise "a judgment a real person made." A matchbox wrapper from two hundred years ago and a UI screenshot today are collectible for the same reason: behind each stands a specific person who made a specific judgment.

### [Dunes Workshop](https://dunesworkshop.org/info)

![Source: Unsplash](../_images/工作难找，不要甩锅给大环境%20-%20No.26-沙丘.jpg)

An online creative and research group founded in 2019 by grad students from Harvard GSD + MIT's School of Architecture. Two core projects: *The Dune Lexicon* (design/art/architecture concept entries) + *Reflecting on the Internet* (critique of space in the digital age). Their work has shown at the Venice Biennale, UCCA Edge, and Ars Electronica; their writing has appeared in *Time + Architecture*, *Urban China*, and *Architectural Journal*.

It's an example of a body of work in the "artisan collective" mold — not taking orders to make goods, but continuously producing work that transcends the algorithm. That lines up exactly with the kind of creators [Dan Koe](https://thedankoe.com/) describes: social media is merely a container to draw attention to your work; attention and money are byproducts of doing good work.
