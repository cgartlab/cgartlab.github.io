# Continuing from Part 1

This is a classic work that deeply explores the process of writing film and television scripts. The author reveals the key to crafting compelling, emotionally resonant stories through detailed techniques on plot construction, character development, and dialogue writing. It was also my first book on screenwriting, and there are so many points worth recording and reflecting on. I'm sharing them here, and I hope you find them helpful.

# 3. Conflict

## The Source of Action

- Every effect has a cause. There is no spontaneous action without reason.

## Cause and Effect

- Every intensifying conflict should be foreshadowed by its determining forces before it occurs.
- The small conflicts leading to the big ones all follow the drama's premise. Small conflicts are "transitions" that continually change the character's mental state until they are forced to make a decision.
- The seeds of conflict exist in everything. No matter how humble a character, they will always have their own goals — even small ones that can trigger escalating conflict.
- Conflict takes many complex forms, but they all evolve from the pattern of "attack" and "counterattack."
- On the surface, a reasonable conflict contains two opposing forces. At their root, they are products of quantitative change. The tension created by these two forces can only be resolved through the full outbreak of conflict.

## Static Conflict

- A character appears on stage, introduces themselves, and exits — solely because the author needs to introduce a new character at that moment. They re-enter the story at the author's will, express some thoughts and feelings, then disappear again to make room for other characters.
- Even if a scene's dialogue is particularly interesting, if it does not advance the conflict, the story stagnates. Only conflict guarantees more conflict. After consciously initiating the first conflict, you must push relentlessly toward the drama's premise, generating conflict after conflict.
- A scene has one main premise. Each character also has their own premise. These collide with one another, and together all the characters drive the story toward the overarching premise.

## Jumping Conflict

- The audience must be fully informed about the characters. But in jumping conflict, the audience only sees surface information. The author must create opportunities for the real character to reveal their inner self, and give the audience a chance to observe the significant changes happening to them. **The technique should not be刻意 or obvious. Trust the audience to discover it on their own.**
- Whenever conflict slows down, jumps, or stalls, the author needs to revisit the premise. Is it clearly stated? Does it still hold? Correct any errors promptly, then return to studying the characters.
- Drama is not an imitation of life, but a reflection of life's essence.
- The protagonist is the most important force in conflict. They enable other characters to grow through conflict. Ensure the protagonist is unshakable — they cannot and will not compromise.

## Escalating Conflict

- Escalating conflict results from a clear premise, three-dimensionally complete characters, and a tight unity of opposites between them.
- As long as three-dimensionally complete characters are given a well-defined premise, any type of conflict has value. Through conflict, characters naturally reveal their traits, take on their roles in the story, create suspense, and keep the story charged with tension.
- In daily life, people argue endlessly about the same things year after year, never truly resolving the root cause of the conflict. In drama, this must be distilled. There is no need for lengthy dialogue to build the impression of accumulated conflict.

## Action

- Every conflict contains an attack and a counterattack, each unique. As the conflict progresses, there are many subtle, almost imperceptible movements that determine what type of escalating conflict the author will use.

## Foreshadowing

- Foreshadowing in drama is essentially a guarantee of future events — a promise of conflicts yet to come.
- In life, people find it hard to trust strangers. Only through conflict can others truly understand you and "prove yourself." Because only in conflict does one's true self emerge.
- Perfect manners and clever words do not prove a person's sincerity and integrity — but sacrifice does.
- Most people wear masks in real life. Everyone is interested in seeing what people do when forced to reveal their true character under the weight of conflict. This truth beneath the mask holds a fatal attraction for the audience.

## Point of Attack

- There must be something urgent — even a matter of life or death — that forces the character to make a decision and act. The character's actions must be necessary to them personally to drive the story forward.
- A scene can open at the critical moment of a conflict. It can also begin at a turning point in a character's life, or with a decision that sparks conflict.
- A good point of attack presents an event at its most critical moment right from the start. It must be the moment of decision, or the moment just before a decision is made.

## Transition

- Daily life and nature skip no steps. There is no jumping conflict in life.
- Even if some transitions exist only briefly — and only in the character's mind, unnoticed even by themselves — they still exist and should be shown. Transition is the lifeblood that keeps drama authentic.
- Transitions connect seemingly unrelated elements: winter and summer, love and hate.
- The audience does not need to see every single action in a character's transformation. It does not need to be described in exhaustive detail — but it must be described.

## Crisis, Climax, and Resolution

- A crisis is a point where a decisive change is about to occur, pushing events in a new direction.
- Nature has no beginning or end. So the opening of a drama is not the beginning of conflict, but the climax of some prior conflict. A character makes a decision, acts on it, initiates escalating conflict, and eventually creates a new crisis and climax.
- Even the shortest scene should contain all the elements of a three-act play. It must have its own premise, revealed through conflict between the characters.

# 4. Summary

## Obligatory Scene

- This is the scene a play must have. It is the one everyone anticipates, the scene the entire play builds toward. Its importance surpasses every other scene.
- A script can only "arouse the highest expectations in the audience" when it demonstrates its premise. The proof of the premise occurs in this obligatory scene.
- Only scenes rooted in the overall narrative can make the audience anticipate the next scene. The emotional intensity of each subsequent scene will always be stronger than the last.

## Exposition

- The audience can actually understand a character's nature through their goals. The author does not need to fully display their emotions or other information — those details will be embedded in the story's development, and the character will reveal them in the process of proving the premise.
- Exposition is a natural, continuous process integrated into the entire script. It is not something that must be explained at the beginning of the story, and it should flow uninterrupted until the end.

## Dialogue

- Dialogue is the primary means of proving the premise, revealing character, and advancing conflict. It is the most直观 part of a scene for the audience.
- Dialogue is only crucial and effective when every line naturally and freely reveals the character's traits and the events happening to them.
- Choose words carefully. Writing is about selection — unlike photography, if the author needs to convey a point, extraneous information must not interfere.
- Dialogue must fit the character's identity. If necessary, the author should sacrifice their own "brilliance" for the character. No clever line is worth sacrificing a character you have created.

## Experimentation

- Every great artist has broken sacred rules that once seemed insurmountable in their industry.
- Learn from nature.
- Necessity will, of course, make characters do things they would never normally do.

## Timeliness of Drama

- As long as the story is told well, every script is relevant to its time. Describing and reflecting the essence of human life is always valuable.
- If writing about a past or future era, you must know that era inside and out.

## Entrances and Exits

- If the author cannot handle a character's entrances and exits, it means they do not know the character well enough.
- A character's appearance or departure should only happen when the plot demands it, and their actions must advance the conflict and reveal their character.

## Why Do Some Bad Plays Still Succeed?

- Such scripts have obvious flaws, but they still contain a premise, conflict, and character arrangement. The author writes about characters the audience already knows, which compensates for weak character development. It is the audience's active participation that makes the characters feel alive and real to them.
- These plays make the audience feel superior — but the fatal flaw is that the characters do not grow or change.

## Steps for Writing a Script

- First, find the premise you want to prove.
- Choose a protagonist to drive the conflict.
- Present each character, orchestrate them carefully, and establish the unity of opposites between them.
- Choose the precise point of attack, ensuring it is a major turning point in the character's life.
- If the conflict can steadily escalate, the crisis will develop into a climax.
- After the climax, ensure the unity of opposites between characters is strong enough that their will to continue developing is not weakened. Otherwise, the characters might abandon the conflict mid-play. Every character must have urgent matters to deal with. The stronger the unity of opposites between characters, the better they can prove the drama's premise.
- Let dialogue originate from the characters, not the author.

## How to Get Ideas and Inspiration

- Even the most ordinary-looking person has had intense impulses. The author's task is to capture the character's most激烈 inner moment.
- When the audience watches a story, they see reflections of their own cruelty, violence, abuse, and animalistic impulses — even if only for a brief moment.
- Only characters who have reached a turning point in their lives are worth writing about. They can serve as warnings and inspiration.
